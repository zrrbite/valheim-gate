## Before the Beginning

Nothing in that world could make its own light.

This is the first thing to know, and most of the people in this story never knew it, or knew it so
long ago that they had forgotten it was a thing that could be known. The ones who remembered did not
talk about it. It is the kind of fact that is easier to live beside than to say out loud.

There are nine worlds, the old tales tell us, held in the branches and the roots of the great ash.
They do not mention the tenth. The tenth is where Odin puts things when he is finished with them:
the dead who did not die well enough for his hall, the beaten, the put-away, the gods who lost. It is
a store-room. It is the place behind the hall where the broken benches go.

Everything in the tenth world was *after*. The grey things in the forests were deadwood that had been
told to stand up. The swamps were full of men who had declined to lie down. The mountains had frozen
solid when whatever kept them warm ran out, and the plains were a ruin somebody else had built and
nobody remembered building. Dead things do not make light. They can only take it.

There was one exception, and it was a herd of deer.

Eikthyr's animals carried the old light, sparks from before the tenth world was a prison. They banked
it by day, the way a careful man banks a fire, and they could only give it up in the dark. When one
of them died at night, its light rose out of it like breath on a cold morning and burned in the open
air for as long as it takes to say a short prayer. Then it went out, or something took it.

Something always took it. The forest was very hungry, and it had been hungry for a long time.

And into that world, one grey morning, the sea put down a living person.

## The Stolen Light

*Something is taking the light from the meadows. Take it back.*

### The Shore

The newcomer woke on the grass at the edge of the water, and they were cold, and wet, and breathing,
and the breathing was the strange part.

They did not know where they were. They did not know that nothing in that place had drawn a breath in
an age, or that the first new light the tenth world had seen since anyone could remember was lying
on its back in the meadow, coughing up seawater. They knew only that they were alive, and that the
dark had noticed.

The dark notices everything. That is what it is for.

Somewhere at the back of their head, in the place where you decide things a moment before you know
you have decided them, an old voice was setting out terms. It had one eye, that voice, and a great
deal of patience, and it did not introduce itself because it did not need to. It would lend, it said.
Whatever the newcomer needed, a wheel would turn and offer, and they could take, and none of it would
be theirs. Death would come and collect a piece of it now and then. At the end, all of it would go
back.

*Power must be earned here.*

It was not a welcome. Odin does not do welcomes. It was a ledger, opening, and a line drawn under the
first page.

So the newcomer built, because building is what the living do when they do not yet know what else to
do.

An axe first. The trees gave up their wood with a sound that went on a little longer than it should
have, as if the forest at the meadow's edge had heard the first stroke and was listening for the
second. Then a hammer, and timber, and stone. Ten pieces of wood became a workbench, and standing in
the circle of it the newcomer made a tunic out of hide, and felt, absurdly, more like somebody.

The meadows were thick with greylings and boar. The newcomer ate the boar and kept a camp, and in the
long afternoons they walked the grass picking up everything they had not held before. Raspberries. A
fat yellow mushroom. A flint. A feather. A knuckle of resin. Twelve new things, and it mattered that
they were new, because the world was teaching its inventory one object at a time, and it did not
count the same lesson twice.

Walls are not a shelter, so the newcomer raised a roof. Under the roof they laid a fire, on the earth
and never under wood, and over the fire they set a spit. When they ate three different things at
once, meat and berries and mushrooms, warmth went down into them and stayed. Then they built a bed
under the roof and lay on it once, and claimed it.

That was the moment the place became theirs. A hearth is where a living spark tends itself, and in a
world that takes and takes, it was the one square of ground that took nothing back.

At night the meadows whispered. They did not whisper loudly. They did not need to.

> "The dark is awake."
>
> "The forest is counting."
>
> "Something is owed, and it is being collected."

The newcomer lay under their roof and listened to the grass say it, and did not sleep for a long
time.

### The Graves

In the morning there was a man standing among stones a little way from the door. He had not been
there in the dark.

He was dead, plainly, and not much troubled by it. He was grey-green and dim in the daylight, lit
from somewhere underneath the way old moss is lit when the sun goes through it. The stones around him
were seven, set in a careful row, and he stood among them as a steward stands in a hall that still
has to be swept, though nobody is coming to eat.

> "Seven stones. I know whose. Come and I will tell you."

The newcomer went. He spoke as though he had rehearsed it for a very long time and had perhaps never
once been asked.

> "I buried them. Seven of a hird that came before you, and I am what is left of the eighth — kept
> here to count the stones, since nobody came for any of us.
>
> Eydís, who hunted, and whom the beasts followed. Sigrún, who mended, and called up what the ground
> held. Ulfr, who never learned how to stop. Halvard, who stood where he was put and did not move.
> Ormr, who sang the rest of us onward. Ragna, who was never once afraid of water. Dvalinn, who built
> the hall they all died in.
>
> None of them needs the name any more. Take one up and I will teach you what they knew — a little
> at a time, as the gods fall. Or take none, and go. The stones are kept either way."

The thane had no name of his own. That was not an accident. What he kept was other people's names,
and a man who keeps other people's names does not have room for one.

The newcomer stood a long while over the hunter's stone. Then they said her name.

> "Eydís, then. She walked with a wolf at heel and nothing saw her go. The wolf comes when you call
> now. The rest of her comes as the gods fall."

And it did. The newcomer whistled, the way she must once have whistled, and a wolf came out of
nothing at all and fell in at their heel as if it had been waiting. A bow sat easier in their hands.
Her quiet came into their feet.

This was not like the one-eyed god's lending, and it is worth saying why. What Odin gave, Odin could
take back; death would come, now and then, and collect one of his gifts and leave a hole where it had
been. What the thane gave was not lent. It was a name the dead had finished with, picked up off a
grave and worn. Death could take anything Odin advanced. It could not take a way.

### The Raven

The newcomer lived. That was the first errand, though nobody had set it.

They sat under their own roof by their own fire, near their bench and their bed, until the room
stopped being a room and became a home. They slept a whole night through and woke with something new
in their legs, a tirelessness, earned the honest way, by resting. They set a chair by a table and a
table by the chair. They built a chest, for the day the world would hand them more than they could
carry. They improved the workbench twice.

And all the while a raven had been circling.

It had been circling since the morning of the shore. It was patient in the way only something that is
not alive can be patient, and one afternoon the newcomer stood still long enough for it to come down
onto a fencepost and look at them with Odin's eye.

Ravens are Odin's thought and Odin's memory. They fly out over all the worlds in the morning and come
home to his shoulders at night and tell him what they saw. They witness. That is the whole of what
they do. They have never once in all the tales helped anybody, and they did not start now.

> "He did not send you for the antlered one. He sent you to find where the light is going."

It was the whole saga in a sentence, and it was the first time anybody had said it. The raven did not
stay to be asked what it meant.

### The Kill That Gave Nothing

The meadows were full of deer, and the deer were beautiful in the ordinary way of animals, and in
another way besides, one the newcomer could not have named: as though something under the hide were a
little too bright for the body it was in.

The newcomer took one at noon, cleanly. It was warm. It gave venison.

It gave nothing else.

> "Nothing rose. They bank it while the sun is up — there is no light in a deer at noon. Come back
> when it is dark."

So they sat in the dark.

There was nothing to kill and nobody to talk to. *Nothing you want walks in the light. Sit in the
dark until you believe it.* They stayed out past sundown in the long grass with no fire, and the
meadow did what it did every night, which was speak. Three times, quietly. The newcomer did not
understand it, but they listened, and listening was the point. When the grey dawn came, they had a
torch they had not had the night before, and eyes that had learned the grain of the dark.

### The Pale Light

Then, one night, something at the edge of the world.

*A pale light kindles ahead.*

It hung over the grass a long way off, small and steady and not a fire. There was no path to it, only
a rumour, a sense of *there*. The newcomer walked towards it the way you walk towards a sound you are
not sure you heard. The grey things in the treeline watched them pass and did not follow. It was not
theirs. It never had been.

The newcomer walked into it, and it went out, and the raven was on a branch above them as if it had
always been.

> "So you found one still burning. There were more, once. Watch what the forest does with the rest."

Then the knowing came, all at once, the way the true shape of a room comes when somebody finally lights
the lamp.

> "It was one of the herd's — the one the forest never found."
>
> "The deer give up their light when they fall, and the forest is always collecting. Hunt after
> dark, and take back what you can."

So that was the shape of it. The herd carried the old light, and gave it up only in the dark, and only
in dying; and the forest, which was starving, which had been starving as long as it had been a
forest, came for every one.

### The Hunter's Shade

*Something waits near your bed at night. It was a hunter once.*

It stood a few strides from the newcomer's bed after dark, pale and faint and wearing the same shape
the lights wore. It was cold blue, and drained, and it spoke before it was seen.

> "You. Closer. I have waited long enough to be quiet about it."

> "I hunted this herd before you. The forest took me with the bow unstrung, and a hunter who never
> loosed is a poor thing to be for an age.
>
> Bring me what I lacked — ten flint and five scraps of leather, the quiver I never filled — and I
> will show you how the bow is strung. Come after dark. I walk in nothing else."

The newcomer brought it what it lacked. Ten flint and five scraps of leather, laid at its feet like an
apology somebody else owed. The shade looked at them for a long time. Then it gave the newcomer a shape
to keep in their head, and the one light it had kept for itself through its whole age of waiting, and
it told them where to take both.

> "Good. Now mark it: wood for the stave, resin to seal it, the herd's own hide for the wrap, and
> this flint of yours for what it looses.
>
> But no bench will strike it. It wants the sky, and there is one who waits on the shore when it
> rains who tends the only thing that can give it one. Take him a light — he will want one.
>
> Men will call it Thor's. Let them — the storm in it is the herd's, and Eikthyr's own, turned.
> What you loose from it, you owe the herd a clean shot."

### The Race

Eikthyr would not come down for a living stranger for nothing. His trophies were the price of an
audience, and his trophies meant his herd. So the newcomer hunted the herd, and in hunting it did
exactly what the forest did. The only difference, and they held onto it the way a drowning man holds
onto a plank, was what they did with the light.

A deer fell in the dark and its light rose out of it and burned. The newcomer had the space of a short
prayer. So did the forest.

They came at once, a pack out of the trees, and more walking in from the black beyond, not towards
the newcomer but towards the light. They were not cruel. That was the worst of it. Every grey thing out
there was a splinter of something enormous and starving under the roots, and a splinter that is not
fed goes dark. They wanted the light the way the drowning want air.

*Its light rises. Take it before they do — walk into it.*

The newcomer ran into it with the drums of a god's own war in their blood, and it went into them, and
the forest howled.

Some nights a light kindled that no deer had died for: a stray, one the forest had not caught yet.
Those came quietly, with no pack, and the newcomer walked up to them as to something sleeping and took
them gently. Five lights, in the end. The newcomer kept count, and so did the forest; it was the one
honest scoreboard in the world. If the forest had taken eight, the hunt would have ended with nothing
in the newcomer's hands. It did not take eight.

Every light made them brighter, and they could feel it, and so could everything else. The tenth world
had begun to turn its face towards them, the way a field of barley turns towards wind. That turning
had a name, though nobody had said it yet. Later the saga would call it heat, and write it beside the
newcomer's name, and multiply their glory by it.

Glory, in that world, was a measure of how brightly you were willing to burn, with everything watching.
It is not a bad measure. It is not a safe one either.

### The One Who Waits by the Water

*When the rain comes there is someone standing at the water's edge. He has been there since before
the herd, he is not one of the forest's, and he has a name.*

You will know this one. Everybody does.

Once, the tales tell us, Thor came down to a farmer's house with his goats, and killed them, and cooked
them, and let the farmer's family eat the meat, on the one condition that they did not break the bones.
And the farmer's son was hungry, and cracked a thigh-bone for the marrow. In the morning Thor raised
the goats with his hammer, and one of them limped. The price of a limp was the boy. His name was
Thjalfi, and he served the Thunderer from that day on.

The tales stop there. They do not say what became of him after. This is what became of him.

The newcomer went down to the shore in the rain. He stood facing the water, larger than a man and gold
where the shade was blue, gold the way old coin is gold under mud. He did not turn.

> "You walk like someone who has taken something. Come here."

> "I broke a bone once. That is the whole of it — a goat's hind leg, snapped for the marrow, because
> I was hungry and it was lying there and I did not think anyone counted bones.
>
> He counted. He took me instead of the goat, and payment does not get to stop being payment just
> because the debt has gone old.
>
> So I know exactly what you are. You have been taking light that something else was counting. The
> difference between us is not the taking. It is that you still have somewhere to put it."

Thjalfi had a name because somebody had once come for him and written it down. The shade had no name;
nobody had come for the shade. Neither of them ever spoke of the other. They did not need to.

He wanted twenty stone, and one of the lights, carried in the newcomer's own hands. Not left in a chest
at home. Carried. *He wants paying, and what he does with it is the only reason he is still here.*

The newcomer paid.

> "Stone for the body. A light for the eye.
>
> Stand back.
>
> There. It is not a forge — there is no fire in it, and nothing in this world can make one worth
> the name. It only breaks. But put in a shape it knows and what it breaks is the shape's own edges,
> and what stands up out of it is the thing you meant.
>
> Put in anything else and you get ash. I have been getting it wrong for a very long time."

The sky cracked like a thigh-bone, and the Storm-Anvil stood on the shore at his feet, steaming in the
rain.

It was a small Elder, if you thought about it, and the newcomer did think about it: the only other
thing in the world that broke light instead of carrying it, and the only one a living hand could own,
and feed, and pull the lever on. They fed it wood and resin and deer hide and flint, and three of the
lights they had risked their life to take back, and they pulled the lever under the storm.

*The herd paid for this in hide, and the sky will pay the rest. Owe them a clean shot.*

Thor's bow came out of the anvil hissing. Its arrows went into the world, and the world answered them
with lightning.

### The Breaker

> "Something came out of the forest, and it is not collecting."

The newcomer knew it by the trees. They moved. Something huge was coming through them that did not
go around anything.

> "That one was never fed. It stopped carrying light a long time ago, and started breaking it."

A troll. A splinter that had never reached the Elder's table, forgotten so long it had stopped being
hungry and started being something worse. *The forest sent nothing for this one. It comes for the
light itself, and it does not collect.* It walked straight at the newcomer's hearth.

And then a strange thing. The grey ones came out of the treeline after it, screaming, and for the
length of one fight the forest was on the newcomer's side.

> "The forest turns on it. They are not helping you — there is just something here they hate more.
> Stay clear of both."

The newcomer put it down well away from the roof, and kept its hide. That night the shade was waiting
by the bed again.

> "Stay a moment. I have been thinking about what you did."

> "You put it down. Good.
>
> It was one of theirs once — a splinter nothing ever came to collect, so it stopped carrying light
> and started breaking it. That is what the forest does to the ones it forgets. Something smaller
> forgot me, and it took longer.
>
> Keep the hide. Whatever the sky did to that thing, it stopped caring a long age ago, and the hide
> is where it stopped caring. Bound over a meadow frame, with light under the boss, it will do the
> same for you — but no bench will bind it either. That wants the shore, and the rain, and three of
> your lights."

So: the shore again, and the rain again, and the anvil, and an exact bill, because the anvil burned
whatever it did not recognise. *The storm is his. Bind something that does not care.*

What came out was the Stormward, and it was the largest thing a living hand had ever held in that
world. It took both hands. It took the bow and the torch and the blade. In return, when something hit
it twice inside a few heartbeats, it gave the storm back: a ring of Eikthyr's own weather breaking
outward. *It keeps what it is hit with. Let something hit it twice, and stand still.*

The storm ate the shield a little every time. That was its price. It kept sending the newcomer home to
their own bench, which is to say, to their hearth, which is to say the shield was wiser than it looked.

One night they stood out under a thunderstorm with no roof over them and the Stormward on their arms,
because *the sky does this on its own, and has since before you landed. Go and be under it holding
what you made.*

They did not feel brave. They felt, for the first time, as though they belonged to the weather.

### The Herald and the Gatherer

The herd had a guardian, a deer that was more than a deer, with two stars on its hide. The newcomer
hunted it for days. When it fell, at night, its light went up like a bonfire, the loudest thing the
meadows had ever let go of, and the whole plain took a breath.

> "The meadows heard that."

Something else heard it too. *The Gatherer knows what you have taken.*

This was the oldest splinter, grown fat enough to earn a name. All along it had followed the hunt at a
distance and let its kin do the dangerous carrying, and every light the newcomer had ever let the
forest have had gone, in the end, to it. It came at night, and it came as the newcomer's own hunt had
made it.

> "The Gatherer comes lean and furious. You left it nothing."

They had left it nothing. It was the proudest sentence anyone had said to them since the sea put them
down. The newcomer killed it, and every light it had ever hoarded went up around its body like sparks
from a kicked fire, free, and theirs to gather in their own time.

### Eikthyr

His altar was two standing stones ringed with runes, on a rise above the grass. The newcomer hung the
trophies his own herd had paid for, and called.

He came down out of a sky that was suddenly full of him: antlers like a burnt forest, lightning in the
hollows of his ribs. Whether he looked down and saw a thief, or the only creature in his country that
had fought the real thieves, the saga does not say. Gods only speak when they are beaten, and he did
not speak.

He went out.

And as he fell something came into the newcomer, the way it had been promised at the graves. The raven
said it and the air said it, and they could not have missed it if they had tried.

> "The way of the Hunter deepens."

The rest of Eydís came a step closer. The wolf had company now, a whole menagerie that came to the
newcomer's call, and Thor's bow learned to turn its storm to fire and to frost.

When the newcomer passed the graves again, the thane looked up from his stones.

> "Nothing more yet. What they knew came to them slowly, and it comes to you the same way. When a god
> falls, the rest will find you where you stand."

The shade came one last time, after dark, the way it always came.

> "You called him down, and he came, and he is gone.
>
> I hunted his herd a whole season and never once saw him. That is the difference between us and it
> is not skill — I never had a reason worth his coming. Keep the bow. The storm in it was his. Now it
> is only yours."

There was a book that kept the saga, and it wrote things down after they were finished, in the past
tense, the way a book does. It addressed the newcomer directly, which books in that world tended to
do. It wrote:

> *Eikthyr came down onto stones his own herd had paid for, and went out. You walked away carrying
> light this world had already lost once — the first of it ever to come back. The forest went on
> being hungry. Nobody had yet asked where it had been carrying everything it took.*

That was the end of the first tale. It was not the end of anything else.

## Where the Light Goes

*The forest has been fed for years. Meet what did the feeding.*

### Under the Pines

> "The little ones carry it somewhere. Nobody has ever followed them home. Odin would like that
> corrected."

The raven said it from the antlers the newcomer had hung on the sacrificial stones, as they claimed
what was left of Eikthyr: a stag's stride, a long low run that ate the ground. Odin would like that
corrected. That is how Odin asks for things. He does not say please. He says what he would like, and
waits, and remembers.

The Black Forest begins all at once. One step there is grass and sky; the next there are pines so tall
and so close that noon is a rumour, and rock, and moss on the rock, and things moving in the moss.
*The Gatherer was only a hand. Everything it collected went this way, for years.* The newcomer walked
into the dark under the trees, and a few heartbeats after the last of the meadow light fell behind
them, the place told them its premise, once, so that they would not forget it.

> "Nothing here makes its own light. Remember that."

They put Eikthyr's antler to the mottled outcrops, forty strokes, and copper and tin came out of the
stone, and coal. They hung a trophy on a wall, because a hall should remember what it has done. They
raised a raft, because the Elder does not always wait on your shore. They raised a cart, heavy and
creaking and hateful on hills, because *goods have to move. The couriers know it, and so does the
trader.*

The newcomer did not know yet what couriers were. They would.

### The Burial Chambers

> "The forest did not take every light. Some were carried down and buried, and the dead have been
> keeping them lit ever since."

The chambers were stone doors set into hillsides, ringed by standing stones, and inside them it was
green. Green fire burned in the walls in small hollows, steady and cold, the colour of something that
has been burning for longer than anybody living has had a name. The dead walked the passages. The
newcomer killed them, and dug the cores out of the walls. They needed ten. The smelter would want
them.

When the newcomer came out into the grey light, somebody was waiting at the door.

He was ash. That was the first impression: a man the colour of a fire's leavings, drained of everything
except a sharp green glow somewhere deep inside him, the same green as the walls.

> "You took from the walls. Come here."

> "You have been inside. I know, because the walls are dimmer.
>
> Those were ours. Every green fire down there is a light somebody buried rather than let the little
> ones carry it off. We could not keep ourselves, so we kept those, and we have kept them lit for
> longer than this forest has had a name.
>
> Burn them, if you must. The living need fire; I remember that much. But bring one back — one of
> the lights the little ones are carrying. They run at night. Cut one loose, carry it down to me, and
> I will give you what the storm left buried here."

The newcomer stood there with ten stolen fires in their pack and could not think of one thing to say.

They built the smelter anyway. *The cores burn as well in a smelter as in a wall. Know what you are
burning.* They knew. Copper and tin went in and bronze came out, the first thing in that whole forest
anybody had made instead of taken, and they forged three things in it, and every one of them was warm
from a dead man's lamp.

This is the kind of thing the old tales do not dwell on, and should. Every bright thing in the world is
paid for by somebody, and mostly it is somebody who was not asked.

### The Couriers

> "The forest moves its harvest at night."

> "Watch for the couriers — one of them will be carrying it, and the light warms whatever holds it.
> Cut that one down and its cargo rises."

First a way had to be cleared. The shamans kept to the nests and healed whatever the newcomer wounded,
and the nests died without them, so the newcomer went for the shamans. Then the Brutes came, slow and
huge and unflinching.

> "The big ones are not stronger. They are FED. The oldest trees feed first, and so do the oldest
> splinters."

The newcomer felled three of them, where there was room to give ground, and understood the forest a
little better for each one. Fed. Everything there was a mouth, and the size of a thing was only a
measure of how much it had been given.

And then the nights.

They came hurrying through the dark, starred and named: Couriers of the Elder. They glowed. The brand on
them was the cargo. Each was a greydwarf given a burden and a title, carrying a light that had once
been somebody's, down some road under the pines to wherever it was going.

*The courier falls, and its cargo rises. Walk into it.*

It was the meadows' race turned inside out. Then, the newcomer had raced the forest to a light. Now
the light was the forest's, already taken, already on its way, and the newcomer was the thief on the
road. When a courier fell its kin came to carry the light onward, and the newcomer got there first.
Four times. Four lights cut off the supply line.

They carried one of them down to the burial chamber.

> "One light. Carried down, not dug up."

The keeper took it from their hands with a care that hurt to watch.

> "There. It will burn longer down here than it would have burned in him.
>
> The storm left a piece of itself in this forest, long ago, and we buried it with the rest. Bronze,
> troll hide and coal, at a forge. Strike it and it is yours."

*The storm gave you a shield. That was the first piece. There is one waiting in every land left.*

At the forge the newcomer struck the Stormsworn helm out of bronze and troll hide, and it sat on their
head as if it had been waiting for one. It answered everything in that forest that swings.

They built a portal of fine wood and greydwarf eyes and surtling cores, and then its twin, and stepped
from one dark to another. *The couriers walk every mile of their road. You need not.* They broke the
black earth with a cultivator and sowed ten seeds, and felt strange doing it: putting something into
the ground, in that place, instead of taking something out.

### The Trader

> "Somebody in this forest has watched the couriers longer than you have. He sells things, at the
> forest's edge."

He kept a camp in a clearing with his lamps turned low: a big, laughing trader with too many rings and
a cart full of things nobody in a starving world should have been able to sell. His name was Haldor.
He did not move. As the newcomer came near, they heard him talking, half to them and half to the
trees.

> "They come past every night. Little ones, glowing like lanterns. Never once stop to trade."

> "Funny thing. They carry more than they could ever eat, and they never look at my wares."

> "I keep my lamps low after dark. Not for the little ones. For the big ones."

That was half the answer to the mystery of him. A trader in a starving forest is never robbed, because
the splinters carry light and have no use for goods. The only thing out there he was afraid of was the
one thing that breaks light instead of carrying it. The newcomer had met one. In the meadows, the whole
forest had come out of the trees to scream at it.

> "Want to know where the light goes? I've watched them for years. It'll cost you a troll's head."

So the newcomer went into the deep forest and found a troll and took its head, and came back, and held
the head up where Haldor could see it.

> "Ha! That one won't be knocking my camp over. Fair's fair, then. Every night they go the same way —
> deeper, to where the trees are oldest. There's a ring of stone in there, and something under it
> that's never once come up to collect. I've marked it on your map."

And there it was, on the map, a mark that had not been there a breath before. Not handed down by a god.
Bought, from a frightened man with his lamps turned low.

The act was answering its own question in pieces, the way a forest gives up a path. The couriers had
shown the newcomer the light *carried*. The keeper had shown them the light *kept back*. Haldor had
shown them *where* it was carried. There was only one thing left to see.

### The Elder

*Follow where the stolen light was carried — the oldest trees feed first. A ring of stone, guarded.*

The oldest trees were enormous, so old their bark had gone to stone in places, and the ground between
them was soft with a hundred years of needles. In the middle of them was the ring Haldor had promised.
The newcomer did what the herd's trophies had taught them to do, and called down the thing the
couriers had been feeding all those years.

It came up out of the ground: a hunger wearing a tree. Roots like hands. A face in the bark that had
never smiled, because smiling is not hunger. It had never once come up to collect in person, and now
it had come up for the newcomer.

It burned.

> "The way of the Hunter deepens."

The last of Eydís arrived, the way the last word of a long sentence arrives, and the newcomer could
walk unseen now, the way she had.

At the burial chamber the keeper was waiting, as if he had felt it go.

> "Whatever he was fed went out with him. The forest is darker for it.
>
> Not down here. Ours are still burning."

And the book wrote:

> *The Elder burned, and everything it had been fed went out with it. So that was where the light
> had gone: nowhere. Spent on keeping deadwood standing. The world was darker than the day you
> landed, and you had helped.*

The newcomer read that sentence many times over the years. *And you had helped.* It did not get easier
to read. That is how you can tell it was true.

## Nothing Stays Buried

*What the marsh takes, it keeps.*

### The Grey Country

> "Men dug here before you, and iron is all they left. Take the iron. Leave the rest where it lies."

The newcomer hung the Elder's trophy where they had hung Eikthyr's, and took his gift, the quick bite of
an axe through wood, because there would be a great deal of wood between them and the iron. They built
a fermenter, because the raven's advice had a warning folded inside it, and because everyone who had
ever gone into the swamp unready was still in it.

The swamp is flat and flooded and grey, and it smells of everything at once. Trees stand up out of the
water like the ribs of drowned boats. Leeches wait in the shallows. Blobs of something that had once
been something else roll along the paths, and where the dead had been left, they were not left long.
The newcomer cleared the mire, and learned quickly that killing a thing in that country was a
suggestion rather than an ending.

> "The swamp does not let go of its dead."

Some of what they put down got up again, right where it had fallen, as bone. Once. After that it stayed
down. Once was enough to teach the newcomer the place.

### The Witch

There was one living thing in the marsh, and she had built her house over the water.

She was old in the way a well is old. She smelled of smoke and nettles and something sweet underneath,
and she sold what she sold, and she watched the newcomer come up to her without getting up from her
cauldron. When she spoke it was to the marsh as much as to them, the way Haldor spoke to his trees.

> "Nothing rots here. Did you notice? Nothing lets go long enough to rot."

> "The marsh keeps everything. The iron, the water, the men. Even its smell."

The newcomer asked her, eventually, how anyone could live in such a place.

> "I live here because nothing here can keep hold of me. I don't let it."

Every witch in every tale is the same witch, in the end, and it is never a good idea to ask her how
old she is. The newcomer did not ask. They asked about the deep water instead, and the thing in it that
everybody who came this far went to meet.

> "The big one is decided before you ever see him. Poison, child. Brew against it, or don't go."

*She said it plainly: the big one is decided before you ever see him.* So the newcomer stirred a base
for poison mead at a cauldron and set it to ferment, and did not argue with her about it, which was
wise. Nobody has ever won an argument with a witch, and the ones who believe they have are mostly
frogs.

Her shop stayed hers. The newcomer bought from it like anyone.

### The Drowned One

*Men dug here before you. The iron they left is the only thing down there still worth carrying.*

The sunken crypts sit in the water like tombs that sank to their shoulders. The newcomer opened them
with the Elder's key and went down into the mud and the green dark and hauled out scrap iron by the
armful: muddy, heavy, wonderful.

At the nearest door, standing in the shallows, there was a draugr.

He did not move when the newcomer came up out of the water with iron in their arms. That was how they
knew. A draugr comes at you; it is what draugr are for. This one stood at the door the way a sentry
stands when he no longer remembers what he was told to guard, and the water around his ankles was very
still. He was pale, bleached by water rather than rotted, with a cold grey-green light in him like the
light at the bottom of a pond.

> "Iron. You came out with iron."

> "You came out of there with iron. I watched you go in.
>
> I have held this door since before the iron in it rusted. Not because there is anything left
> behind it worth holding. Because holding is all the marsh lets anyone do. Nothing here is let
> go of. Not the iron, not the water, not us.
>
> What I wore is no use to me now. The storm made it, a long time ago, for a man who meant to come
> back out. Take it to a forge — iron and guck and leather — and wear it better than I did."

So the newcomer smelted. Ten bars of iron, enough to stand in, out of a smelter fed with coal at home.
Then a forge, improved, and twelve iron and six guck and ten scraps of leather, and the Stormsworn
cuirass came off the anvil dark and heavy and smelling of the marsh. *Third piece. The men who left
this iron in the water did not have one.* The newcomer put it on, and the fen stopped getting into them.

> "The coat first."

They went back to him wearing it. It was dusk, the marsh's long grey dusk, when the light goes out of
the water before it goes out of the sky.

> "Now. Before my hand remembers."

> "You wore it out of here. Good.
>
> Now the other thing. Put me down. I have tried to let go of this door for longer than you have
> been alive, and my hand will not open. Yours will.
>
> I will lift the blade when you come. I always do. Do not let that stop you."

He lifted the blade. He always did. There was not much left of him, and he did not fight as draugr
fight. He fought the way a man does a thing out of habit, a little behind himself. The newcomer did not
let it stop them.

When he fell, the swamp did not take him back. Everything else the newcomer had killed in that country
had been offered a second standing, and some of it had taken the offer. Not him. He lay down in the
shallows at the door he had held for an age, and stayed down.

*He let go. Something he kept rises where he fell.*

It was small and steady and warm, and it was his: the one light he had never let go of, rising in the
dark over the water. The newcomer walked into it, and it went into them, and for a moment they had the
strangest feeling, of a hand opening.

### The Shield Made Heavier

> "That shield of yours has a storm in it. Bring it to me with iron and old bark, and I'll make it heavier."

The newcomer sailed the fens, a long way inland on black water, where the crypts sit on their shores
and the trees lean over to look at you. Then they went back to the witch with the Stormward on their
back, the great storm-shield of the meadows, scarred by everything that had ever hit it; and ten bars
of iron; and ten slabs of the ancient bark the swamp's oldest trees wear like mail.

*The storm's first piece, made heavier by the one person in the marsh who lets go of things.*

She did not use a forge. Whatever she used, she did not explain, and the newcomer did not ask, for the
reasons already given. When she handed it back it was darker and heavier, banded with iron and backed
with bark that had outlived kingdoms, and it still hummed with Eikthyr's weather when they tapped it.

> "There. Iron where the hide was, bark where the frame was, and the storm still in it. It will hold
> longer than you will. Most things here do."

The Ironbound Stormward. The first craft of the meadows, remade in the country that keeps everything.

### Bonemass

The newcomer felled an Abomination, a great walking knot of roots and drowned men that rose out of a bog
like a hill standing up. *Roots and drowned men, standing up together. This is what holding on looks
like.* It was wood as much as it was meat, and fire and a heavy blunt edge undid it.

Then the newcomer drank the witch's answer, and went looking for a skull on a mound deep in the mire
where the water hides the path, and called down everything that had never let go.

He came up out of the water, a heap of the dead fused into one enormous patience, poison from his skin
to his heart, exactly as she had said. The newcomer had drunk before they came. It made all the
difference she had promised.

He came apart in the water he had stood in since before the herd.

> *Bonemass came apart in water it had stood in since before the herd. Nothing in the marsh had been
> carrying light anywhere. It had simply been kept — and that was the first hoard you had found that
> nobody was using.*

Kept. Not spent, like the Elder's. Not carried. Only held, for nothing, by things that had forgotten
how to open their hands. The newcomer thought of the drowned one at his door, and the light that rose
when he finally fell, and thought that they were beginning to understand what the one-eyed god had
sent them to find.

They did not like it. That is generally the way with understanding.

## The White Silence

*Above the treeline, even light freezes.*

### The Treeline

> "Nothing up here is hungry. That is worse. Hunger at least wants something."

The newcomer claimed what Bonemass had left them, on the same stones, a hide that turned aside blunt and
slash and pierce, because the mountains would ask for all three. They drank frost mead and wrapped
themselves in warm skins. And they climbed.

The mountains begin at the treeline. Below it the last pines lean into the wind. Above it there is
nothing, a white that goes up and up into a sky the colour of an old knife, and a silence so complete
you can hear your own heart deciding whether to go on.

The book put it better, afterwards, the way books do.

> *Above the treeline nothing moved and nothing rotted and nothing was hungry. The cold had been
> holding what it took for so long that it had stopped behaving like a thief at all, and you climbed
> up to see what it had underneath.*

### The Frozen One

He was standing at the treeline. Exactly at it, as if he had been climbing and the cold had reached out
and stopped him mid-stride, one foot still on the last of the grass.

He was white all through, frost-white, and he did not move when the newcomer spoke to him.

> "He does not move. Ice over his eyes, ice in his beard, and under it all, very deep, something
> faintly warm. A fire might reach him."

The newcomer almost walked on. Then they saw it: deep inside him, under all that white, the way you
sometimes see a coal still alive under a morning's ash, a faint warm amber glow.

Something was still lit in there.

They built a fire. There was no reason to, and every reason. They cut wood from the last of the trees
and laid a fire beside him and lit it, and the warmth went out towards him the way warmth goes, and
slowly, so slowly that the newcomer thought they were imagining it, the ice on his face began to weep.

> "...is it back?"

His voice was a crack running across a frozen lake.

> "Is it back?
>
> I came up to see it come back. When the light started going, below, I said it would come back, and
> I would climb to where you can see the whole world at once and watch it. I got this far. This is
> where the cold starts. I did not know it started so low.
>
> No — do not tell me it is gone. Gone is a word for things you have seen go. Up there the great one
> lays her eggs: light that has not woken yet. Bring me one. I want to hold one before I believe you."

### The Egg

*Nothing up here has moved in an age. Whatever the mountain was keeping, it is under your feet.*

First the newcomer went down into the mountain, with Bonemass's wishbone twitching in their hand over the
places where silver lay, and dug it out of the rock: fifteen ore, cold enough to hurt through gloves.
They hunted the white silence, wolves in packs and the long-limbed fenrings that come out at night and
come downhill. Boulders that were not boulders unfolded beside them and stood up. *They were set to
watch something. It has been gone a very long time. Nobody told them.* The newcomer killed two, and
wondered what they had been watching.

Then they climbed to the peaks.

The eggs lay in nests of bone and frost on the highest, windiest places, as if their mother wanted them
as close to the sky as rock would allow. They were bigger than a man's chest. They were warm. Not
much, not enough to melt the frost they lay in, but warm, the only warm thing for a mile, and something
moved inside them very slowly, the way a sleeper turns over.

> "One of hers. From the peaks, where it is coldest. It is heavy, and it will not go through any door
> you have built — you will have to carry it down."

So the newcomer carried it. Down off the peak and down the ice, down through the fenrings' slopes and
the wolves' valleys, down and down to the treeline, one step and then another, with the egg pressed to
their chest and their arms shaking and their breath smoking in the air. They knew, the whole way, that
the great one would need three of these before she would come down to be fought, and that they were
carrying one of them away. They carried it anyway.

That is the kind of choice the tales are full of, and the tales are right to be. You can tell a great
deal about somebody by what they will carry down a mountain for a stranger.

> "One of hers. Please."

He had not moved. The fire had burned low. The newcomer built it up again and knelt and set the egg in
his frozen hands.

> "It's warm. It's — warm.
>
> Then it isn't gone. Not all of it. Take what the storm made for the cold; I never needed it,
> standing still. Silver, wolf pelt and fang, at a forge built high."

So the newcomer raised their forge high, and struck the Stormsworn greaves out of silver and wolf, and
they closed round their legs like a promise kept. *Fourth piece, and the cold is the easiest thing it
will ever have to keep out.*

When they looked back from the trail he was still standing at the treeline with the egg in his arms
and the fire at his feet.

> "I'll keep the egg warm. Somebody should."

### Hildir

Far below, where it was warm, a woman kept a camp of bright tents and good cloth, and the newcomer went
to her because the cold had got into their bones and they wanted to stand somewhere warm and buy
something soft.

Hildir sold clothing. She was brisk and bright-eyed, and she talked like someone who has travelled a
great deal and decided that most of it was weather.

> "The cold doesn't steal, you know. It keeps. Nothing up there rots, and nothing up there is used."

> "I keep my camp where it's warm. I send other people up into it. That's the trick of it."

She had sent people up. She was not ashamed of it. There is a kind of person who goes into the
dangerous places, and a kind who sells them warm clothes beforehand, and both kinds are necessary, and
only one of them gets eaten by wolves.

> "Something of mine is up in the Howling Cavern. The cold has it now. It doesn't want it — it just won't give it back."

> "Wolves on the slopes, and worse things in the caves. Dress for it."

So the newcomer dressed for it, and went up into the Howling Cavern, a hole in the mountain where the
wind screamed through the rock and the cultists of the cold kept fires that gave no heat. Past the
fenrings, past the cultists, into the deepest chamber, where her chest stood frozen to the floor:
perfect, exactly as she had said, kept, and of no use to anybody. The newcomer broke it loose and
carried it all the way back down to the warm.

She opened it, and smiled, and her smile was the first warm thing the newcomer had seen in a long time
that was not a fire.

### Moder

On the highest bone-ringed peak, cold enough to kill without a mead, the newcomer found Moder's altar.

They had climbed for eggs again. They had to. Each time the eggs were warm against their chest, like
something asking them not to. They laid three on the altar, and she came down out of her own sky.

She was enormous and white and beautiful, the queen of the cold, and she fought for her unwoken light
the way anything fights for its young. Frost poured out of her. The peak went white and then whiter.
The newcomer stood in it in the Stormsworn and the wolf skins and the frost mead, and did not freeze,
and did not stop.

She fell out of her own sky.

And the mountain gave up what it had been keeping. It came up out of the ice all around the peak, out of
the frozen rock and the frozen lakes and the frozen bodies of the golems who had watched it so long, a
slow breath of something that had been held in place for an age. The newcomer felt it go past them into
the white air.

It was warm.

That was the thing they would remember. Not her size, or her fury, or the moment she broke. That it was
still warm. Kept so long and so perfectly that it had never once had the chance to cool.

At the treeline, far below, somebody had felt it too.

> "Did you feel it go past? Up off the peaks, when she fell. It was warm. I was right.
>
> It did not help me. Go on."

And the book wrote:

> *Moder fell out of her own sky and the mountain gave up what it had been keeping. It was still warm.
> Whatever is up there is not taking light to use it, and that was worse than any answer you had had
> so far.*

The newcomer stood on the peak with the wind tearing at them and looked south, past the mountains, past
everything, to where the land went flat and gold and endless under a sky that seemed, from up there,
almost to have been harvested.

Something down there had tried to answer the shortage before them. You could see the shape of the
answer from the summit, the way you can see the shape of a field from a hill: long, straight, deliberate
lines, cut into the whole of the country.

The raven landed on the altar stone and said nothing at all.

---

## The Golden Ruin

*They harvested a god's herd before you. See how it ended.*

### The Plains

> "Look at the stones before you fight anything. Someone tried exactly what you are trying."

The newcomer claimed what Moder had left them, on the same stones as always: a following wind,
whichever way they sailed. By now the sacrificial stones had five trophies on them, and the newcomer had
stopped noticing that they were the trophies of gods. That is what happens. It is the first thing that
happens to anyone who kills enough gods, and it is the one the tales are most careful never to mention.

Then they went down into the plains.

The grass there grows tall and gold and moves all of a piece in the wind, like the coat of a vast
animal. Deathsquitos come out of it before you hear them. Lox graze in it, slow and enormous, and hit
like a cart. Fulings camp in it, small and quick and cruel, and they had built their huts on top of
everything without once asking what it had been for.

> *Somebody had done all of this before you. The plains were built out of their leavings - their
> stones, their walls, their harvest - and the fuling had put their huts on top without ever asking
> what any of it had been for.*

The newcomer broke the plains, which is to say they killed a great many fulings and deathsquitos and a
few lox, and learned the country by what it threw at them. They killed two Fuling Berserkers, two-handed
and unstoppable, and took the totems from what was left.

*They are still keeping a quota. Nothing has been harvested here in an age, and nobody has told them that
either.*

That was the thing about the fulings, once you saw it. They were not the harvesters. They were what the
harvesters had left in charge.

### The Harvester

The raven had said to look at the stones before fighting anything, and so the newcomer looked at the
stones.

They stood in rings out on the plain: great grey megaliths, taller than houses, set by people who had
known exactly what they were doing. Lines ran out from them across the country, straight as a ruler,
old ditches and old walls and old roads, all of them pointing at something that was no longer there.

At the nearest ring, somebody was standing in the grass.

He was a ghost, but not like the others. The shade had been cold blue and the thane moss-green and the
frozen one white with a coal inside him. This one was gold. Dull gold, the colour of grain that has
stood too long in the field and gone grey at the ears. He did not look at the newcomer when they came.
He was looking out along one of the long straight lines, the way a man looks at a field he is about to
reap.

"You're the one," he said, "who has been taking it back."

The newcomer said they supposed they were.

"We did that," the harvester said. "Before you. Not the way you do it. You do it one at a time, in the
dark, with your hands. We did it all at once." He lifted one grey-gold hand and drew a line along the
horizon, and every one of the old walls and ditches lay along it, as if his finger had cut them. "We
took the herd. We took the forest, and what the forest had taken. We cut the whole country into lines
so that nothing could fall between them, and we brought it here, and stored it, and we were very proud.

"We never once sat down. That is the part they don't put on the stones. There was always more to take
in, and there was always a reason not to stop yet, and somewhere in the middle of all that taking we
forgot what the taking had been for. The little ones out there are still counting it." He nodded at the
fuling smoke along the grass. "They don't know it's over. Nobody told them. Nobody told us, either. We
found out the way you find out a well is dry. By dropping the bucket."

The newcomer asked him what he wanted. They had learned by then that the dead in that world always
wanted something, and that it was usually the one thing they could no longer do for themselves.

"Eat," said the harvester.

### A Meal From the Field

He meant it simply, and it was not simple.

"Not from the store," he said. "Not something you took. Something the field grew. Bread from the
barley. A pie from the lox. Fish in a wrap of good flatbread. Eat until you can't, with your belly full
of the field, and come and stand where I can see you. I want to see someone eat what grows here instead
of harvesting it. Just once. Before the ground forgets what it was for."

So the newcomer became a farmer, for a while, which nobody in any of the old tales ever bothers to
become until the end.

They broke the plains' ground and sowed barley and flax in it, which grow nowhere else. They built a
windmill on flat open ground, stone and wood and sail, and it turned in the plains wind and ground the
barley to flour. *Barley and flour for the last feast.* They baked bread. They hunted a lox, which is
like hunting a hillside, and made a pie of it. They ate until their three hungers were full and warm,
the meadows' first lesson come round again five countries later, and they went and stood in front of
the harvester in the long grass with crumbs on their chin.

He looked at them for a long time.

"So that's what it was for," he said.

Then he gave them the last thing the storm had made, which his people had harvested along with
everything else and stored and forgotten. Lox pelt and needles and silver, at a workbench of the third
degree. *The last piece. Wear all of it into what is left — that is what it was for.* The newcomer
cured the Stormsworn mantle and settled it on their shoulders, and for the first time wore the whole of
the storm at once: the helm from the forest, the cuirass from the marsh, the greaves from the mountain,
the mantle from the plains, and the great shield from the meadows, made heavier in the swamp.

It was not a set of armour. It was a record of everything the newcomer had survived, worn on the body.
That is what armour always is, if you look at it properly.

### The Sealed Tower

Hildir had a third chest. Of course she did. People who send other people up mountains are not the kind
of people who stop at two.

"The plains," she said, holding up a bolt of cloth against the newcomer's shoulders to see if the colour
suited. It did not. She put it down. "A harvest is a funny thing. It leaves the field so bare you can
see every stone in it, and so full somewhere else that nobody can find anything. My chest is in one of
those somewhere-elses. A tower out on the plains, all shut up. They sealed it because it was full, I
expect. Things that are full get sealed. It's the empty ones you can walk into."

So the newcomer went out to the Sealed Tower, through the fulings who guarded it as if the quota inside
it still mattered, and up and in, and found her chest among a great deal of other things that had been
stored and never once used. They brought it back to her, and she opened it, and did not say thank you,
because she was not that kind of person. She said, instead, that the newcomer had better have the good
cloth after all, and would not let them pay.

### The Feast

Before the end, the newcomer went home.

Not to the plains. Home: to the meadows, to the first roof and the first fire and the first bed, the
square of ground in all that world that had never once taken anything from them. They set the table.
They set it the way the harvester's people never had, with bread from their own barley and meat from
their own hunting and mead from their own fermenter, and they sat down at it, in their own house, in
front of their own fire, and they ate.

That was all. It does not sound like much, told aloud. It is the thing the whole saga had been arguing
for since the morning the sea put them down, and it is the thing every god in it had failed to do.

### Yagluth

*A ruin of stone hands in the tall grass. Fulings camp near it.*

His altar was a ring of carved stone hands rising out of the plain, palms open to the sky, as if the
whole country had once held something up there to be looked at. The newcomer laid five fuling totems
on it, the quota his little ones had been keeping all that time, and called down the last of the
harvesters' gods.

He came up out of the ground the way a reaper comes up a field: Yagluth, who had been the hand that
closed on everything. Fire was in him, and spite, and a terrible tiredness that the newcomer
recognised, because they had seen it before, in a man made of grain-coloured light standing in the
grass at a ring of stones. He had been storing for so long that he no longer knew what for.

The newcomer stood in the whole storm at once, and did not burn, and did not stop.

And when Yagluth fell, his hand closed one last time, on nothing, and stayed shut.

> *Yagluth's hand closed on nothing and stayed shut. The first harvesters were dust in the fields
> they had cleared, and you were standing in their answer. It had not worked for them either.*

The newcomer went back to the ring of stones, afterwards. The harvester was gone. There was nothing in
the grass where he had stood but a little flattened place, as if somebody had sat down there for a
while, at last, and then got up and gone on.

The raven came and sat on one of the megaliths and looked at the newcomer with Odin's eye. It did not
speak. It did not need to. It was counting, as ravens do, and the newcomer knew now what it was
counting, and why.

Everything in that world had answered the shortage in its own way. The forest had stolen, and the
marsh had kept, and the mountain had frozen what it held so long it forgot to use it, and the
harvesters had taken everything at once and never sat down. Every one of the answers had been wrong,
and every one of them had been tried with great conviction by somebody who was certain.

The newcomer had been carrying lights since the first night in the meadows. They had never really
asked themselves what they were doing with them, only that they were not doing what the forest did.
That was the question the one-eyed god had sent them to answer, and it was the one they had not
answered yet.

## What Is Not Yet Told

That is as far as the saga goes, for now.

They say that in the mist beyond the plains there are lamps, and that the lamps were not stolen. They
say a short, stubborn people keep light in lanterns there, and that they borrow it, and give it back,
and will not explain to anyone how. They say the raven has already flown there and come home again,
and that Odin would like to know, very much, from whom the dvergr borrow it.

And some, who have looked carefully at what the newcomer carries, say that they have been doing it all
along.

But that is another tale, and it has not happened yet.
