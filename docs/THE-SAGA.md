## Act I — The Stolen Light

*Something is taking the light from the meadows. Take it back.*

### Ashore

Nothing in this world can make its own light.

You did not know that yet, the morning the water put you down on the grass. You knew only that you
were cold, and wet, and breathing, and that the breathing was the strange part. Valheim is the tenth
world, the one Odin keeps for what is finished: the dead, the beaten, the put-away. Everything that
walks here is walking *after*. The grey things in the treeline were deadwood that had been told to
stand up. The swamps to the east were full of men who had declined to lie down. Somewhere above the
snow, the mountains had frozen solid when whatever kept them warm ran out.

And here you were, alive, on the shore, the first new light the place had seen in an age.

The dark noticed you before anything else did.

There was a voice before there was a world, or it felt that way. Somewhere at the back of your head,
in the place where a decision gets made before you know you have made it, an old one-eyed patience
was speaking terms. It would lend. That was the shape of it: whatever you needed, the wheel would
turn and offer, and you could take, and none of it would be yours. *Power must be earned here.* It
was not a welcome. It was a ledger opening.

So you built, because building is what the living do when they do not yet know what else to do.

An axe first. The trees gave up their wood with a sound that went on longer than it should have, as
if the forest at the edge of the meadow had heard and was listening for the next stroke. A hammer
next, and timber and stone. Ten pieces of wood became a workbench, and standing close to it you made
yourself a tunic out of hide, and felt, absurdly, a little more like somebody.

The meadows were thick with greylings and boar. You fed yourself on the boar and kept a camp, and you
walked the long grass in the afternoons picking up everything you had not held before: raspberries,
a fat yellow mushroom, flint, a feather, a knuckle of resin from a pine. Twelve new things. It
mattered that they were new. The world was teaching you its inventory one object at a time, and it
did not count the same lesson twice.

Walls are not a shelter, so you raised a roof. Under it you laid a fire, on the ground, never under
wood. You set a spit across the fire, not beside it. When you ate three different things at once,
cooked meat and berries and mushrooms, warmth went down into you and stayed, and for the first time
since the water you were properly fed. Then you built a bed under the roof and lay down on it once
and claimed it.

That was the moment the place became yours. The hearth is where a living spark tends itself. In a
world that takes, it was the one square of ground that was not taking from you.

At night, the meadows whispered.

> "The dark is awake."
>
> "The forest is counting."
>
> "Something is owed, and it is being collected."

You lay under your roof and listened to the grass say it, and did not sleep for a long time.

### The graves

In the morning there was a man standing among stones a little way from your door. He had not been
there in the dark.

He was grey-green and dim in the daylight, lit from somewhere underneath, the way old moss is lit
when the sun goes through it. He was dead, plainly, and not much troubled by it. The stones around him
were seven, set in a careful row, and he stood among them the way a steward stands in a hall that
still has to be swept though nobody is coming to eat.

> "Seven stones. I know whose. Come and I will tell you."

You went. He spoke as though he had been rehearsing it for a very long time, and had perhaps never
once been asked.

> "I buried them. Seven of a hird that came before you, and I am what is left of the eighth — kept
> here to count the stones, since nobody came for any of us.
>
> Eydís, who hunted, and whom the beasts followed. Sigrún, who mended, and called up what the ground
> held. Ulfr, who never learned how to stop. Halvard, who stood where he was put and did not move.
> Ormr, who sang the rest of us onward. Ragna, who was never once afraid of water. Dvalinn, who built
> the hall they all died in.
>
> None of them needs the name any more. Take one up and I will teach you what they knew — a little
> at a time, as the gods fall. Or take none, and go. The stones are kept either way."

You stood a long while over the hunter's stone. Then you said her name.

> "Eydís, then. She walked with a wolf at heel and nothing saw her go. The wolf comes when you call
> now. The rest of her comes as the gods fall."

And it did. You whistled, the way she must have whistled, and a wolf came out of nothing at all and
fell in at your heel as if it had been waiting. Her bow sat easier in your hands. Her quiet came into
your feet. It was not like the one-eyed god's lending. What the thane gave you was not lent. It was a
name the dead had finished with, picked up off a grave and worn. Death, you would learn, could
collect anything Odin had advanced you; it would never take a way.

### The errand

You lived. That was the first errand, though nobody had set it.

You sat two minutes under your own roof by your own fire, near your bench and your bed, and felt the
room become a home. You slept through a whole night in the bed and woke with a hot meal waiting and a
fishing rod by the door and something new in your legs, a tirelessness, earned the honest way, by
resting. You set a chair by the table, and then a table by the chair, and the room grew comfortable.
You built a chest, so that you would have somewhere to put things when the world started handing you
more than you could carry. You took the workbench and improved it twice, a chopping block and a
tanning rack inside its circle.

And all the while a raven had been circling. It had been circling since the morning you landed. It
was patient the way only something that was not alive could be patient, and one afternoon you stood
still long enough for it to come down onto a fencepost and look at you with Odin's eye.

> "He did not send you for the antlered one. He sent you to find where the light is going."

It was the whole of the saga in one sentence, and it was the first time anyone had said it. The raven
did not stay to be asked what it meant. Ravens witness. They never help.

### The kill that gave nothing

The meadows were full of deer, and they were beautiful in the ordinary way animals are beautiful, and
in another way too, that you could not have named: as though something under the hide were slightly
too bright for the body it was in.

You took one at noon, cleanly. It was warm. It gave you venison.

It gave you nothing else.

> "Nothing rose. They bank it while the sun is up — there is no light in a deer at noon. Come back
> when it is dark."

So you sat in the dark.

There was nothing to kill and nobody to talk to. *Nothing you want walks in the light. Sit in the
dark until you believe it.* You stayed out past sundown in the long grass with no fire, and the
meadow did what it did every night: it spoke. Three times, quietly. You did not understand it, but
you listened, and listening was the point. When grey dawn came you had a torch you had not had the
night before, and eyes that had learned the dark's grain.

### The pale light

Then, one night, something at the edge of the world.

*A pale light kindles ahead.*

It hung over the grass a long way off, north and east, small and steady and not a fire. There was no
compass bearing for it, only a rumour, a sense of *there*. You walked towards it the way you walk
towards a sound you are not sure you heard. The grey things in the treeline watched you pass and did
not follow. It was not theirs. It never had been.

You walked into it, and it went out.

> "So you found one still burning. There were more, once. Watch what the forest does with the rest."

Hugin, from a branch. Then the knowing came, all at once, the way the true shape of a room comes when
somebody finally lights the lamp:

> "It was one of the herd's — the one the forest never found."
>
> "The deer give up their light when they fall, and the forest is always collecting. Hunt after
> dark, and take back what you can."

The deer carried original light. Sparks from before this world was a prison. They banked it by day
and could give it up only in the dark, and when they died at night it rose out of them and burned for
half a minute in the open air. And the forest, which was starving, which had been starving for as long
as it had been a forest, came for every one.

### The hunter's shade

*Something waits near your bed at night. It was a hunter once.*

It stood a few strides from your bed after dark, pale and faint and wearing the same shape as the
lights. Cold blue, drained. You heard it before you saw it.

> "You. Closer. I have waited long enough to be quiet about it."

> "I hunted this herd before you. The forest took me with the bow unstrung, and a hunter who never
> loosed is a poor thing to be for an age.
>
> Bring me what I lacked — ten flint and five scraps of leather, the quiver I never filled — and I
> will show you how the bow is strung. Come after dark. I walk in nothing else."

You brought it what it lacked. Ten flint and five scraps of leather, laid at its feet like an
apology somebody else owed. It looked at them for a long time. Then it gave you a shape to keep in
your head, and the one light it had kept for itself through all its age of waiting, and it told you
where to take them both.

> "Good. Now mark it: wood for the stave, resin to seal it, the herd's own hide for the wrap, and
> this flint of yours for what it looses.
>
> But no bench will strike it. It wants the sky, and there is one who waits on the shore when it
> rains who tends the only thing that can give it one. Take him a light — he will want one.
>
> Men will call it Thor's. Let them — the storm in it is the herd's, and Eikthyr's own, turned.
> What you loose from it, you owe the herd a clean shot."

### The race

Eikthyr would not answer a living stranger for nothing. His trophies were the price of an audience,
and his trophies meant his herd. So you hunted the herd, and in hunting it you did exactly what the
forest did. The only difference, and you held onto it, was what you did with the light.

A deer fell in the dark and its light rose out of it like breath on a cold morning, and burned. You
had half a minute. So did the forest. They came at once, a pack out of the trees, and more walking in
out of the black from further off, not towards you but towards the light. They were not cruel. That
was the worst of it. Every grey thing out there was a splinter of something enormous and starving
under the roots, and a splinter that is not fed goes dark. They wanted the light the way the drowning
want air.

*Its light rises. Take it before they do — walk into it.*

You ran into it at a sprint with the drums of a god's own war in your blood, and it went into you,
and the forest howled.

Some nights a light kindled that no deer had died for, a stray, one the forest had not caught yet.
Those came quietly, with no pack, and you walked up to them as to something sleeping, and took them
gently. You took back five. You kept count, and so did the forest; it was the one honest scoreboard
in the world. If it had taken eight, the hunt would have ended with nothing in your hands. It did not
take eight.

Every light you took made you brighter. You could feel it, and so could everything else. The world
had begun to turn its face towards you, the way a field of grass turns towards wind. That turning had
a number, though nobody had told you what it was called. Later the saga would call it heat, and
write it next to your name, and multiply your glory by it.

### The one who waits

*When the rain comes there is someone standing at the water's edge. He has been there since before
the herd, he is not one of the forest's, and he has a name.*

You went down to the shore in the rain. He stood facing the water, larger than a man and gold where
the shade was blue, gold the way old coin is gold under mud. He did not turn.

> "You walk like someone who has taken something. Come here."

> "I broke a bone once. That is the whole of it — a goat's hind leg, snapped for the marrow, because
> I was hungry and it was lying there and I did not think anyone counted bones.
>
> He counted. He took me instead of the goat, and payment does not get to stop being payment just
> because the debt has gone old.
>
> So I know exactly what you are. You have been taking light that something else was counting. The
> difference between us is not the taking. It is that you still have somewhere to put it."

He was Thjalfi, and he had a name because somebody had once come for him and written it down. The
shade had no name; nobody had come for the shade. Neither of them ever mentioned the other.

He wanted twenty stone, and one of the lights, carried in your own hands. Not left in a chest at home.
Carried. *He wants paying, and what he does with it is the only reason he is still here.* You paid.

> "Stone for the body. A light for the eye.
>
> Stand back.
>
> There. It is not a forge — there is no fire in it, and nothing in this world can make one worth
> the name. It only breaks. But put in a shape it knows and what it breaks is the shape's own edges,
> and what stands up out of it is the thing you meant.
>
> Put in anything else and you get ash. I have been getting it wrong for a very long time."

The sky cracked, and the Storm-Anvil stood on the shore at his feet, steaming in the rain. It was a
small Elder, if you thought about it, and you did think about it: the only other thing in the world
that broke light instead of carrying it, and the only one you could own and feed and pull the lever
on yourself. You fed it wood and resin and deer hide and flint, and three of the lights you had
risked your life to take back, and you pulled the lever under the storm.

*The herd paid for this in hide, and the sky will pay the rest. Owe them a clean shot.*

Thor's bow came out of the anvil hissing. Its arrows went into the world and the world answered with
lightning.

### The Breaker

> "Something came out of the forest, and it is not collecting."

You knew it by the trees. They moved. Something huge was coming through them that did not go around
anything.

> "That one was never fed. It stopped carrying light a long time ago, and started breaking it."

A troll. A splinter that never reached the Elder's table, forgotten so long it had stopped being
hungry and started being something worse. *The forest sent nothing for this one. It comes for the
light itself, and it does not collect.* It walked straight at your hearth.

And then a strange thing. The grey ones came out of the treeline after it, screaming, and for the
length of one fight the forest was on your side.

> "The forest turns on it. They are not helping you — there is just something here they hate more.
> Stay clear of both."

You put it down well away from your roof, and kept its hide. That night the shade was waiting by
your bed again.

> "Stay a moment. I have been thinking about what you did."

> "You put it down. Good.
>
> It was one of theirs once — a splinter nothing ever came to collect, so it stopped carrying light
> and started breaking it. That is what the forest does to the ones it forgets. Something smaller
> forgot me, and it took longer.
>
> Keep the hide. Whatever the sky did to that thing, it stopped caring a long age ago, and the hide
> is where it stopped caring. Bound over a meadow frame, with light under the boss, it will do the
> same for you — but no bench will bind it either. That wants the shore, and the rain, and three of
> your lights."

So: the shore again, the rain again, the anvil, and an exact bill, because the anvil burned whatever
it did not recognise. *The storm is his. Bind something that does not care.* What came out was the
Stormward, and it was the largest thing a living hand had ever held in this world. It took both hands.
It took your bow and your torch and your blade. In return, when something hit it twice inside a few
heartbeats, it gave the storm back, a ring of Eikthyr's own weather breaking outwards around you.
*It keeps what it is hit with. Let something hit it twice, and stand still.*

The storm ate the shield a little every time. That was its price, and it kept sending you home to
your own bench, which is to say it kept sending you to your hearth. You stood out under a thunderstorm
one night with no roof over you and the Stormward on your arms, because *the sky does this on its
own, and has since before you landed. Go and be under it holding what you made.*

You did not feel brave. You felt, for the first time, like you belonged to the weather.

### The Herald, and the Gatherer

The herd had a guardian. Two stars on its hide and real bearings to it on the wind, a deer that was
more than a deer. You hunted it for days. When it fell, at night, its light went up like a bonfire,
the loudest thing the meadows had ever released, and you heard the whole plain take a breath.

> "The meadows heard that."

Something else heard it too. *The Gatherer knows what you have taken.*

The oldest splinter. Fat enough to have earned a name. All act it had followed the hunt at a distance
and let its kin do the dangerous carrying, and every light you had ever let the forest have had gone,
in the end, to it. It came at night, and it came as your own hunt had made it.

> "The Gatherer comes lean and furious. You left it nothing."

You had left it nothing. It was the proudest sentence anyone had said to you since the water. You
killed it, and every light it had ever hoarded went up around its body like sparks from a kicked
fire, free, yours to gather in your own time.

### Eikthyr

His altar was two standing stones ringed with runes, on a rise above the grass. You hung the trophies
his own herd had paid for, and called.

He came down out of a sky that was suddenly full of him: antlers like a burnt forest, lightning in
the hollows of his ribs. Whether he looked at you and saw a defiler, or the only creature in his
country that had fought the real thieves, the saga never says. Gods only speak when defeated, and he
did not speak.

He went out.

And as he fell something came into you, the way it had been promised at the graves. Hugin said it,
and the air said it, and you would not have missed it this time if you had tried:

> "The way of the Hunter deepens."

The menagerie came to your call. Thor's bow learned to turn its storm to fire and to frost. Eydís
was a little further into you than she had been.

When you passed the graves again the thane looked up from his stones.

> "Nothing more yet. What they knew came to them slowly, and it comes to you the same way. When a god
> falls, the rest will find you where you stand."

The shade came one last time.

> "You called him down, and he came, and he is gone.
>
> I hunted his herd a whole season and never once saw him. That is the difference between us and it
> is not skill — I never had a reason worth his coming. Keep the bow. The storm in it was his. Now it
> is only yours."

Later, much later, you would read it in the chronicle, in the past tense the book keeps for things
that are finished:

> *Eikthyr came down onto stones his own herd had paid for, and went out. You walked away carrying
> light this world had already lost once — the first of it ever to come back. The forest went on
> being hungry. Nobody had yet asked where it had been carrying everything it took.*

## Act II — Where the Light Goes

*The forest has been fed for years. Meet what did the feeding.*

### Under the pines

> "The little ones carry it somewhere. Nobody has ever followed them home. Odin would like that
> corrected."

The raven said it from the antlers you had hung on the sacrificial stones, as you claimed what was
left of Eikthyr: a stag's stride, a long low run that ate the ground. Then you set your face away
from the meadows.

The Black Forest begins all at once. One step there is grass and sky. The next there are pines so
tall and close that noon is a rumour, and rock, and moss on the rock, and things moving in the moss.
*The Gatherer was only a hand. Everything it collected went this way, for years.* You walked into
the dark under the trees, and twelve heartbeats after the last of the meadow light fell behind you,
the place said its premise, once, so you would not forget it.

> "Nothing here makes its own light. Remember that."

You put Eikthyr's antler to the mottled outcrops, forty strokes, and copper and tin came out of the
stone, and coal. You hung a trophy on a wall because a hall should remember what it has done. You
raised a raft, because the Elder does not always wait on your shore. You raised a cart, heavy and
creaking and hateful on hills, because *goods have to move. The couriers know it, and so does the
trader.*

You did not know yet what couriers were. You would.

### The burial chambers

> "The forest did not take every light. Some were carried down and buried, and the dead have been
> keeping them lit ever since."

They were stone doors set into the hillsides, ringed by standing stones, and inside them it was
green. Green fire burned in the walls in little hollows, steady and cold, the colour of something
that has been burning for longer than anybody alive has had a name. The dead walked the passages,
and you killed them, and you dug the cores out of the walls. You needed ten. The smelter would want
them.

When you came out into the grey forest light, someone was waiting at the door.

He was ash. That was the first impression: a man the colour of a fire's leavings, drained of
everything except a sharp green glow somewhere inside him, the same green as the walls.

> "You took from the walls. Come here."

> "You have been inside. I know, because the walls are dimmer.
>
> Those were ours. Every green fire down there is a light somebody buried rather than let the little
> ones carry it off. We could not keep ourselves, so we kept those, and we have kept them lit for
> longer than this forest has had a name.
>
> Burn them, if you must. The living need fire; I remember that much. But bring one back — one of
> the lights the little ones are carrying. They run at night. Cut one loose, carry it down to me, and
> I will give you what the storm left buried here."

You stood with ten stolen fires in your pack and could not think of a single thing to say.

You built the smelter anyway. *The cores burn as well in a smelter as in a wall. Know what you are
burning.* You knew. Copper and tin went in and bronze came out, the first thing in that whole forest
anybody had made instead of taken, and you forged three things in it, and every one of them was
warm from a dead man's lamp.

### The couriers

> "The forest moves its harvest at night."

> "Watch for the couriers — one of them will be carrying it, and the light warms whatever holds it.
> Cut that one down and its cargo rises."

First you had to clear a way. The shamans kept to the nests and healed whatever you wounded, and the
nests died without them, so you went for the shamans. Then the Brutes, slow and huge and unflinching.

> "The big ones are not stronger. They are FED. The oldest trees feed first, and so do the oldest
> splinters."

You felled three, where there was room to give ground, and understood the forest a little better
for each. Fed. Everything here was a mouth, and the size of a thing was only a measure of how much it
had been given.

And then the nights. They came hurrying through the dark, starred and named, Couriers of the Elder,
and they glowed. The brand on them was the cargo. Each one was a greydwarf given a burden and a title,
carrying a light that had been somebody's, down some road under the pines to wherever it was going.

*The courier falls, and its cargo rises. Walk into it.*

It was the meadows' race turned inside out. In Act I you had raced the forest to a light. Now the
light was the forest's, already taken, already on its way, and you were the thief on the road. Their
kin came to carry it onward when a courier fell, and you got there first. Four times. Four lights cut
off the supply line.

You carried one of them down to the burial chamber.

> "One light. Carried down, not dug up."

The keeper took it from your hands with a care that hurt to watch.

> "There. It will burn longer down here than it would have burned in him.
>
> The storm left a piece of itself in this forest, long ago, and we buried it with the rest. Bronze,
> troll hide and coal, at a forge. Strike it and it is yours."

*The storm gave you a shield. That was the first piece. There is one waiting in every land left.*

At the forge you struck the Stormsworn helm out of bronze and troll hide, and it sat on your head as
if it had been waiting for one. It answered everything in that forest that swings.

You built a portal of fine wood and greydwarf eyes and surtling cores, and then its twin, and stepped
through from one dark to another. *The couriers walk every mile of their road. You need not.* You
broke the black earth with a cultivator and sowed ten seeds, and felt strange doing it, putting
something into the ground here instead of taking something out.

### Haldor

> "Somebody in this forest has watched the couriers longer than you have. He sells things, at the
> forest's edge."

He kept a camp in a clearing, lamps turned low, a big laughing trader with too many rings and a cart
full of things nobody in a starving world should have been able to sell. He did not move. As you came
near you heard him talking, half to you, half to the trees:

> "They come past every night. Little ones, glowing like lanterns. Never once stop to trade."

> "Funny thing. They carry more than they could ever eat, and they never look at my wares."

> "I keep my lamps low after dark. Not for the little ones. For the big ones."

That was the answer to the mystery of him, or half of it. A trader in a starving forest is never
robbed, because the splinters carry light and do not want goods. The only thing out there he was
afraid of was the one thing that breaks light instead of carrying it. You had met one. In the
meadows, the forest had come out of the trees to scream at it.

> "Want to know where the light goes? I've watched them for years. It'll cost you a troll's head."

So you went and found a troll in the deep forest and took its head, and came back, and held the head
up where he could see it.

> "Ha! That one won't be knocking my camp over. Fair's fair, then. Every night they go the same way —
> deeper, to where the trees are oldest. There's a ring of stone in there, and something under it
> that's never once come up to collect. I've marked it on your map."

And there it was, on your map, a mark that had not been there a breath before. Not handed to you by a
god. Traded for, by a frightened man with his lamps turned low.

The act was answering its own question in pieces, the way a forest gives up a path. The couriers had
shown you the light *carried*. The keeper had shown you the light *kept back*. Haldor had shown you
*where* it was carried. There was only one thing left to see.

### The Elder

*Follow where the stolen light was carried — the oldest trees feed first. A ring of stone, guarded.*

The oldest trees were enormous, so old their bark had gone to stone in places, and the ground between
them was soft with a century of needles. In the middle of them was the ring Haldor had promised. You
did what the herd's trophies had taught you to do and called down the thing the couriers had been
feeding all those years.

It came up out of the ground: a hunger wearing a tree. Roots like hands. A face in the bark that had
never once smiled because smiling was not hunger. It had never come up to collect in person, the
chronicle would say, and now it had come up for you.

It burned.

> "The way of the Hunter deepens."

Unseen, the last of Eydís. You felt her finish arriving, the way a long sentence finishes.

At the burial chamber the keeper was waiting, as if he had felt it go.

> "Whatever he was fed went out with him. The forest is darker for it.
>
> Not down here. Ours are still burning."

And the book wrote it down:

> *The Elder burned, and everything it had been fed went out with it. So that was where the light
> had gone: nowhere. Spent on keeping deadwood standing. The world was darker than the day you
> landed, and you had helped.*

You read that sentence many times. *And you had helped.* It did not get easier.

## Act III — Nothing Stays Buried

*What the marsh takes, it keeps.*

### Into the grey

> "Men dug here before you, and iron is all they left. Take the iron. Leave the rest where it lies."

You hung the Elder's trophy where you had hung Eikthyr's and took his gift, the quick bite of an axe
through wood, because there would be a great deal of wood to cut through to reach the iron. You built
a fermenter with honey and thistle, because the raven's advice had a warning folded inside it, and
because everyone who had ever gone into the swamp unready was still in it.

The swamp is flat and flooded and grey, and it smells of everything at once. Trees stand up out of
the water like the ribs of drowned boats. Leeches wait in the shallows. Blobs of something that had
once been something else roll along the paths, and where the dead had been left, they were not left
long. You cleared the mire. You learned, quickly, that killing a thing in that country was a
suggestion rather than an ending.

> "The swamp does not let go of its dead."

Some of what you put down got up again, right where it had fallen, as bone. Once. Then it stayed down.
Once was enough to teach you the place.

### The Bog Witch

There was one living thing in the marsh, and she had built her house on stilts over the water.

She was old in the way a well is old, and she smelled of smoke and nettles and something sweet
underneath, and she watched you come up her ladder without getting up from her pot.

"Look at you," she said. "Wet to the knee and frightened of a smell. Sit. Nothing here will hurt
you that isn't already dead, and nothing dead comes up my ladder."

You asked her, eventually, how she could live in a place like that.

"Nothing rots here," she said. "Did you notice? Wood in water a hundred years and it's still wood.
Men in water a hundred years and they're still men, near enough, and still walking. It isn't
preserved. It just won't let go. The water holds on to everything, and everything holds on back." She
stirred. "I live here because nothing keeps hold of me. I make sure of that every morning."

Later, when you had been back enough times that she had stopped watching your hands, she said the
thing you had really climbed her ladder for, though you had not known it.

"The big one in the deep water. You'll go to him. They all go to him, the ones who come this far.
Listen: he is decided before the fight, not in it. He is poison, all of him, and you can't outrun
poison and you can't outlast it. You can only have drunk the answer before you get there." She
nodded at the fermenter you had dragged here at such cost. "Stir a base. Let it sit. Don't argue
with me about it."

You stirred a poison mead base at her cauldron, honey and thistle and the neck of something that
lived in the water, and set it to ferment. She watched you do it, and for the first time something
like approval crossed her face.

Her shop stayed hers. You bought from it like anyone.

### The drowned one

*Men dug here before you. The iron they left is the only thing down there still worth carrying.*

The sunken crypts sit in the water like tombs that sank to their shoulders. You opened them with the
Elder's key and went down into the mud and the green dark and hauled out scrap iron by the armful,
muddy, heavy, wonderful.

At the nearest door, standing in the shallows, there was a draugr.

He did not move when you came up out of the water with iron in your arms. That was how you knew. A
draugr comes at you; it is what they are for. This one stood at the door the way a sentry stands when
he no longer remembers what he was told to guard, and the water around his ankles was very still. He
was pale, bleached by water rather than rotted, and there was a cold grey-green light in him like the
light at the bottom of a pond.

> "Iron. You came out with iron."

> "You came out of there with iron. I watched you go in.
>
> I have held this door since before the iron in it rusted. Not because there is anything left
> behind it worth holding. Because holding is all the marsh lets anyone do. Nothing here is let
> go of. Not the iron, not the water, not us.
>
> What I wore is no use to me now. The storm made it, a long time ago, for a man who meant to come
> back out. Take it to a forge — iron and guck and leather — and wear it better than I did."

So you smelted. Ten bars of iron, enough to stand in, out of a smelter fed with coal at home. Then
the forge, improved, and twelve iron and six guck and ten leather scraps, and the Stormsworn cuirass
came off the anvil dark and heavy and smelling of the marsh. *Third piece. The men who left this iron
in the water did not have one.* You put it on, and the fen stopped getting into you.

> "The coat first."

You went back to him wearing it.

> "Now. Before my hand remembers."

> "You wore it out of here. Good.
>
> Now the other thing. Put me down. I have tried to let go of this door for longer than you have
> been alive, and my hand will not open. Yours will.
>
> I will lift the blade when you come. I always do. Do not let that stop you."

He lifted the blade. He always did. There was not much left of him, and he did not fight the way
draugr fight; he fought the way a man does a thing out of habit, a little behind himself. You did
not let it stop you.

When he fell, the swamp did not take him back. Everything else you had killed in that country had
been offered a second standing, and some of it had taken the offer. Not him. He lay down in the
shallows at the door he had held for an age and stayed down, and where he lay, a light rose.

It was small and steady and warm, and it was his. The one he had never let go of. You walked into it,
and it went into you, and for a moment you had the strangest feeling, of a hand opening.

### The Stormward, reforged

You sailed the fens, six hundred metres of black water inland, where the crypts sit on their
shores and the trees lean over to look at you. Then you went back up the witch's ladder carrying the
Stormward on your back, the great storm shield from the meadows, scarred now by everything that had
hit it, and ten bars of iron, and ten slabs of the ancient bark that the swamp's oldest trees wear like
armour.

She looked at the shield for a long while.

"Meadow hide," she said. "And light under the boss. That's lovely work, that is, for a place where
nothing has to last." She ran a thumb along its edge, where the storm had eaten it. "It'll last here.
Give it to me."

You gave it to her, and the iron, and the bark. She did not use the forge. She used her hands, and her
pot, and something she muttered that was not quite a song, and when she gave it back it was heavier,
darker, banded with iron and backed with bark that had outlived three kingdoms, and it still hummed
with Eikthyr's weather when you tapped it.

"There," she said. "Now it can hold on to things too. Mind you don't let it."

The Ironbound Stormward. The first craft of the meadows, remade in the country that keeps everything.

### Bonemass

You felled an Abomination, a great walking knot of roots and drowned men that rose out of a bog like
a hill standing up. *Roots and drowned men, standing up together. This is what holding on looks
like.* Fire and blunt weapons. It was wood as much as it was meat.

Then you drank the witch's answer, and went looking for a skull on a mound deep in the mire, where
the water hides the path, and called down everything that had never let go.

He came up out of the water, a heap of the dead fused into one enormous patience, and he was poison
from his skin to his heart, exactly as she had said. You had drunk before you came. It made the
difference she had promised it would.

He came apart in the water he had stood in since before the herd.

> *Bonemass came apart in water it had stood in since before the herd. Nothing in the marsh had been
> carrying light anywhere. It had simply been kept — and that was the first hoard you had found that
> nobody was using.*

Kept. Not spent, like the Elder's. Not carried. Just held, for nothing, by things that had forgotten
how to open their hands. You thought about the drowned one at his door, and the light that rose when
he finally fell. You thought that you were starting to understand what the god had sent you to find,
and that you did not like it.

## Act IV — The White Silence

*Above the treeline, even light freezes.*

### The climb

> "Nothing up here is hungry. That is worse. Hunger at least wants something."

You claimed what Bonemass had left you, on the same stones, a skin that turned aside blunt and slash
and pierce, because the mountains would ask for all three. You drank frost mead and wrapped yourself
in warm hide. And you climbed.

The mountains begin at the treeline. Below it, the last pines lean into the wind. Above it there is
nothing, a white that goes up and up into a sky the colour of an old knife, and a silence so complete
you can hear your own heart deciding whether to keep going.

The chronicle would say it best, later:

> *Above the treeline nothing moved and nothing rotted and nothing was hungry. The cold had been
> holding what it took for so long that it had stopped behaving like a thief at all, and you climbed
> up to see what it had underneath.*

### The frozen one

He was standing at the treeline. Exactly at it. As if he had been climbing and the cold had simply
reached out and stopped him mid-step, one foot still on the last of the grass.

He was white all through, frost-white, with ice in his beard and ice over his eyes, and he did not
move when you spoke to him, and he did not move when you touched his arm, and the ice was thick. You
almost walked on.

Then you saw it. Deep inside him, under all that white, the way you sometimes see a coal still alive
under a morning's ash, there was a faint warm amber glow.

Something was still lit in there.

You built a fire. There was no reason to, and every reason. You cut wood from the last trees and laid
a fire at his feet and lit it and sat beside it, and the warmth went out towards him the way warmth
goes, and slowly, so slowly you thought you were imagining it, the ice on his face began to weep.

"Is it back?" he said.

His voice was a crack running through a lake.

"I came up to see it come back. When the light started going, below, I said it would come back. I
said I would climb to where you could see the whole world at once, and watch it come back. I got this
far." He looked down, very slowly, at the grass under his foot. "This is where the cold starts. I
didn't know it started so low."

You told him. You told him about the meadows and the forest and the marsh, the light carried and
spent and kept. He listened with the fire hissing on his frozen boots.

"No," he said. "No. It isn't gone. Gone is a word for things you have seen go. I haven't seen it go.
Up there" — his eyes moved, only his eyes, towards the white peaks — "up there the great one lays her
eggs. Light that hasn't woken yet. Light that hasn't started. Bring me one. I want to see one before
I go. I won't believe it's gone until I have held one that isn't."

### The egg

*Nothing up here has moved in an age. Whatever the mountain was keeping, it is under your feet.*

First you went down into the mountain, with Bonemass's wishbone rattling in your hand over the places
where silver lay buried, and you dug it out of the rock, fifteen ore, cold enough to hurt through
your gloves. You hunted the white silence: wolves in packs, and the long-limbed fenrings that only
came out at night and only came downhill. Boulders that were not boulders unfolded beside you and
stood up. *They were set to watch something. It has been gone a very long time. Nobody told them.* You
killed two, and wondered what they had watched.

Then you climbed to the peaks.

The eggs lay in nests of bone and frost on the highest, windiest places, as if their mother wanted
them as close to the sky as rock would allow. They were bigger than your chest. They were warm. Not
much, not enough to melt the frost they lay in, but warm, the only warm thing for a mile, and
something moved in them very slowly, the way a sleeper turns over.

You took one. It weighed as much as a person. It would not go through a portal; the old magic would
not carry a thing so full of unstarted light. So you carried it, down off the peak, down the ice,
down through the fenrings' slopes and the wolves' valleys, down and down to the treeline, one step and
then another, with the egg pressed to your chest and your arms shaking and your breath smoking.

You knew, the whole way, that the great one would need three of these to come down and face you. You
knew you were carrying one of them away. You carried it anyway.

He had not moved. The fire had burned low. You built it up again and knelt and set the egg in his
frozen hands.

For a long time he only held it. The amber in him brightened, very slightly, as though it could see.

"There," he said. "There. You see? It isn't gone. It's only waiting." He bent his head over the
egg, the ice cracking in his neck. "That's all I wanted. To be right about one thing."

Then he told you what he had been carrying up the mountain when the cold caught him, and where the
smith who made it had worked, and how: silver from the deep rock, wolf pelt for the warmth, wolf
fang for the edge, at a forge raised high. You struck the Stormsworn greaves out of silver and wolf
and they closed round your legs like a promise. *Fourth piece, and the cold is the easiest thing it
will ever have to keep out.*

When you looked back from the trail he was still standing at the treeline with the egg in his arms
and the fire at his feet, as if he meant to keep it warm until it hatched.

### Hildir

Far below, in the meadows where you had started, a woman kept a camp of bright tents and good cloth,
and you went home to her because the cold had got into your bones and you wanted to stand somewhere
warm and buy something soft.

Hildir sold clothing. She was brisk and bright-eyed and practical, and she talked the way people
talk who have travelled a great deal and decided most of it was weather.

"Up the mountain? In that?" She looked at your boots with real pity. "Oh, my dear. The cold doesn't
steal, you know. That's the thing nobody understands about it. A thief wants your purse. The cold
doesn't want anything. It just keeps. Whatever it gets hold of, it keeps — frozen, perfect, forever,
and no use to anyone. Nothing up there rots and nothing up there is used." She shook out a cloak.
"That's why I keep my camp down here where it's warm, and I send other people up."

She had sent people up. One of her chests had gone up into the Howling Cavern, a hole in the mountain
where the wind screamed and the cultists of the cold kept their fires, and it had not come down.

"It's still up there," she said. "Perfect, I expect. Frozen. Kept. Would you? I'd rather have it back
than have it kept."

So you went into the Howling Cavern, into the screaming dark, past the fenrings and the cultists and
their fires that gave no heat, and you found her chest frozen to the floor in the deepest chamber,
perfect, exactly as she said, and you broke it loose and carried it all the way back down to the
warm.

She opened it, and smiled, and the smile was the first warm thing you had seen in a long time that
was not a fire.

### Moder

On the highest bone-ringed peak, cold enough to kill without a mead, you found Moder's altar.

You had two eggs where there should have been three, and you climbed to the nests for another, and
another, the eggs warm against your chest each time like something asking you not to. You laid them
on her altar, and she came down out of her own sky.

She was enormous and white and beautiful, the queen of the cold, and she fought for her unwoken light
the way anything fights for its young. Frost poured out of her. The peak went white and then whiter.
You stood in it in the Stormsworn and the wolf hide and the frost mead, and you did not freeze, and
you did not stop.

She fell out of her own sky.

And the mountain gave up what it had been keeping. It came up out of the ice all around the peak, out
of the frozen rock and the frozen lakes and the frozen bodies of the golems who had watched it so
long, a slow exhalation of something that had been held in place for an age. You felt it go past you
into the white air.

It was warm.

That was the thing you would remember. Not her size or her fury or the moment she broke. That it was
still warm. Kept so long and so perfectly that it had never once had the chance to cool.

> *Moder fell out of her own sky and the mountain gave up what it had been keeping. It was still warm.
> Whatever is up there is not taking light to use it, and that was worse than any answer you had had
> so far.*

You stood on the peak with the wind tearing at your hair and looked south, past the mountains, past
everything, to where the land went flat and gold and endless under a sky that seemed, from up here,
almost to have been harvested.

Something down there had tried to answer the shortage before you. You could see the shape of their
answer from the summit, the way you can see the shape of a field from a hill: long, straight,
deliberate lines, cut into the whole country.

The raven landed on the altar stone beside you and said nothing at all.
